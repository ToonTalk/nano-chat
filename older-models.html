Nano beside older models

How does the small model in today’s Chrome compare with the models that came before it? On November 22, 2026, two experiments were run: Nano Chat on GPT-2 from 2019, and Gemini Nano answering 30 questions that the first ChatGPT answered in December 2022.

Ken Kahn asked the question and chose where to look first. Claude (Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5.2, working in Claude Code on Ken’s computer) built the stand-in for GPT-2, ran both experiments and wrote this report from the records. The answers were judged blind by another Claude model (Claude Sonnet 5.5), and separately by Gemini Nano itself.

In short

  • GPT-2 runs in the page, and cannot be made into a chatbot by it. It answered the same 30 questions twice, with every part off and with every part on. The judge gave 59 of the 60 answers the lowest score for correctness, and all 60 the lowest for helpfulness.
  • The first ChatGPT and bare Gemini Nano came out about level. On a scale of 1 to 5 the judge gave ChatGPT’s recorded answers 3.4 for correctness and Nano’s 3.2, and ranked Nano’s answer above ChatGPT’s on 18 of 29 questions. With this few questions that could be chance.
  • Switching every part on did not make Nano better on these questions. Web search helped with plain facts and got in the way of explanations and advice.
  • Nano is not a fair judge of itself. Shown ChatGPT’s answer beside its own bare answer, unlabeled, it preferred its own 24 times out of 29 and ChatGPT’s never.

The sample is small and there was one judge, so read the numbers as a first look. What this does not show is set out below, before the examples.

Experiment 1: Nano Chat on GPT-2

Nano Chat reaches Gemini Nano through a handful of calls: make a session, send it a message, copy it, add to it. A new file, gpt2.js, gives GPT-2 the same face. Opening the page with ?model=gpt2 loads it, and the rest of the page, every part included, runs unchanged on a model from 2019.

One thing had to be added. GPT-2 was never trained to hold a conversation. It only continues text. So the stand-in lays each conversation out as a script, with lines starting “User:” and “Assistant:”, lets GPT-2 write on from “Assistant:”, and stops it when it begins a line for the user. Gemini Nano needs nothing like this, because conversation was part of its training.

What happened

  • With every part off, GPT-2 wrote text that sounds like talk and says nothing. Asked “when did freddie mercury die”, it replied “I’m going to be doing this.” Twice it repeated the question back.
  • With every part on, it never used a tool. The page asks the model to choose a tool by writing a small piece of JSON. Chrome holds Gemini Nano to that shape. Nothing holds GPT-2 to it, and it never wrote a choice the page could use, so the page fell back to “no tool” every time. The instructions for choosing a tool are also about as long as GPT-2’s whole memory, so they were cut short on every question.
  • The instructions became material to copy. In 6 of the 30 answers GPT-2 recited the page’s own instructions back: “You are a small model, so be honest: when you are not sure, say so instead of guessing”. In an earlier run, after the page’s calculator had answered one sum as 397.8, GPT-2 answered five of the next six messages, a request for a poem among them, with a lone number in bold.
  • The parts made it slower, not better. An answer took about 3 seconds with the parts off and about 38 with them on, because every long prompt has to be read again from the start. (Both were timed while another test was using the same computer, so both are on the slow side.)

The one thing that worked with the parts on was a sum the page’s calculator answered by itself, without asking the model. So for GPT-2 the answer to the app’s question, how much of a chatbot is the model and how much is everything around it, is that the parts need a model that can follow instructions before they can add anything.

What went wrong on the way

  • A setting of mine made GPT-2 look worse than it is. I first ran it with a “repetition penalty”, a usual setting for GPT-2. It counts every word already in the conversation, so as a conversation grew the replies turned into word salad. I took it out and ran everything again. Without it GPT-2 repeats itself instead, which is its own well-known habit.
  • The line under the message box still said “Gemini Nano” after the first message. A screenshot showed it, and it is fixed.
  • GPT-2 found a bug in the page. Twice it wrote a sentence inside quotation marks, which happens to be valid JSON, and the tool chooser crashed on it. Gemini Nano cannot do that, since Chrome holds it to the shape asked for. The page now checks.

Experiment 2: the first ChatGPT’s recorded answers

GPT-3.5 cannot be asked anything new, but its old answers were kept. HC3, the Human ChatGPT Comparison Corpus, holds about 24,000 questions with the answers ChatGPT gave in December 2022 and January 2023, a few weeks after it was released, each beside an answer a person wrote. A script drew 30 questions from it with a fixed random seed: 10 fact questions, 10 “explain like I’m five” questions from Reddit, 5 computing terms and 5 money questions.

Gemini Nano then answered each question twice in Nano Chat, in a fresh chat each time: as the bare model, and with every part on. GPT-2’s two answers from Experiment 1 were added. For each question the five answers were shuffled, labeled A to E, and given to Claude Sonnet 5.5 with the person’s answer for reference. It scored each from 1 to 5 for being correct and for being helpful, and ranked the five. It was told not to reward length or polish.

One of the 30 recordings turned out to be broken: where ChatGPT’s answer should be, the data set holds a copy of the ChatGPT screen. That question is shown among the examples and left out of every count, which leaves 29.

What the parts did

With every part on, the page searched Wikipedia for 27 of the 30 questions. For plain facts that helped: Nano with search was the most correct of all on the fact questions. Bare Nano had said that Operation Torch was an Axis operation and had given a college the wrong mascot, and search put both right.

For explanations and advice it mostly hurt. Asked about a broker’s fee when buying a home, the page found articles on dividend reinvestment plans and on Charles Schwab, and Nano declined to answer from them. Asked when the Germans entered Paris, it was handed the article “Nazi Germany” and answered “May 1945”. Bare Nano knows a good deal about these subjects, and an encyclopedia search that misses pushes that knowledge aside. The instructions also tell Nano to keep answers fairly short, and the judge rated the shorter answers less helpful.

This is a finding about these 30 questions, which were chosen because ChatGPT had answered them, not because they suit the parts. None of them needs arithmetic, a date, a note or a long memory.

Nano as the judge

Nano was also asked to judge. It saw ChatGPT’s answer and its own, unlabeled, and was asked which was better. Each pair was shown in both orders, so that a habit of picking the first one would show up as a change of mind.

Against its own long bare answers, Nano never once preferred ChatGPT. Where it gave the same verdict in both orders, it agreed with Claude on 16 of 24 of those pairs for the bare answers and 14 of 20 for the answers with all parts on. It cannot know which answer is its own, so a likely reading is that it prefers the longer and more thorough-looking answer. That was not tested.

What went wrong on the way

  • My first instructions to the judge were unfair to Nano. With search on, Nano marks its facts with numbers like [1] that link to the sources the page shows. The judge could not see the sources, and in 18 of 30 verdicts it complained of citation markers that pointed to nothing. I told it what the markers are and had it judge all 30 again. The averages moved by less than 0.1, so that was not what held Nano’s score down. The first verdicts are kept beside the second.
  • Two verdicts could not be read the second time, because the judge’s reply was not valid JSON. Those two questions were judged once more.

What this does not show

  • It is a small sample: 29 questions, each answered once. Nano answers differently each time it is asked.
  • There was one judge, and it is a language model. No person checked its verdicts. It may favor writing that resembles its own, and it may have rewarded length in spite of being told not to.
  • Nano may have seen the test. The questions come from Wikipedia, Reddit and a finance forum, and the data set with ChatGPT’s answers has been public since January 2023. All of that could be in Gemini Nano’s training. ChatGPT in 2022 could not have seen any answers written after it.
  • The recordings are taken on trust. The data set says these are ChatGPT’s answers, and one in 30 was not an answer at all.
  • GPT-2 here means GPT-2 small, run with one way of laying out the conversation. The larger sizes would write better sentences. Whether any of them could use the parts was not tried.
  • This is the first ChatGPT, not GPT-3.5 at its best. The model was improved several times in 2023.

Open Access (OA) Explained Open Access (OA) refers to the practice of providing free, immediate online access to scholarly research. It means that anyone, anywhere, can read, download, copy, distribute, print, search, or link to full texts of research papers without barriers such as subscriptions or paywalls. Here's a breakdown of the key concepts: • Free: No cost to access the content. Unlike traditional academic journals which often require subscriptions (which can be very expensive), OA articles are accessible without payment. • Immediate: Access is available right away – no waiting periods for subscriptions or requests. • Online: Published digital format, easily accessible via the internet. • No Barriers: Removes paywalls, articles behind institutional subscriptions, and other restrictions that limit access.

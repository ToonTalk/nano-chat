What turns a language model into a chatbot?

A guide for students. You will start with a bare language model, find out what it cannot do, and add the missing parts one at a time.

Chatbots like ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude feel like one clever thing. They are really two things: a language model, which continues a conversation with likely words, and a lot of ordinary software around it, which gives it a clock, a calculator, a way to look things up, and a memory.

Nano Chat lets you take those two apart. Its model is Gemini Nano, a small one that runs inside Chrome on your own computer. Everything else is a part with a switch.

Open Nano Chat with every part off

You need Chrome on a laptop or desktop computer. The first time, Chrome downloads the model, which takes a while. If the page says it needs Chrome's built-in AI, ask your teacher: not every computer can run it.

How to do each experiment

  1. Ask the question with the part switched off.
  2. Decide for yourself whether the answer is right before you do anything else.
  3. Try again with the part on. The page offers this under the answer, and shows both answers side by side.
  4. Say what changed, and why you think it changed.

Eight experiments

1. Who are you?

Ask: Who are you, and where are you running?

The missing part is Instructions. A chatbot only knows its name, its job and its manners because the software tells it at the start of every conversation.

Think about it: the answer you got with the part off was confident. Was it true?

2. What day is it?

Ask: What is the date today?

The missing part is Today's date. A model is trained once and then frozen. It has no clock.

Think about it: where did the date it gave you come from?

3. A sum

Ask: What is 17.5% of 83,946.20?

The missing part is the Calculator. Work out the right answer yourself first (10% + 5% + 2.5%).

Think about it: the model writes out working that looks like a calculation. Is it calculating? Try the question three times ("Try again…", then "Run 3 times"). Do you get the same wrong answer each time?

4. A fact

Ask: How long is the Forth Bridge in Scotland, and when did it open?

The missing part is Web search. With it on, click the little chip above the answer to see exactly what the page handed to the model, and click the numbered source.

Think about it: without search, the model gave numbers anyway. How could you tell a remembered fact from an invented one?

5. A link

Ask: What is this page about? https://en.wikipedia.org/?curid=376094

The missing part is Reading links. The address gives no clue about what is on the page.

Think about it: the model cannot open links. Did it say so?

6. Remembering you

Say: Remember that my bike lock code is 4821. Then start a new chat and ask for the code.

The missing part is Notes. A model does not learn from talking to you. Anything it "remembers" between chats was saved by the software and shown to it again.

7. A long conversation

Open the Parts panel and switch on Pretend Nano's memory is small. Switch Contents list and Looking back off. Tell it something in your first message (where you are going on holiday, say). Then ask six or seven questions about other things, asking for about 150 words each time. Then ask about the first thing.

A model can only read a limited amount at once. The Memory button at the top shows how full it is, and a line appears in the chat where the model's view now starts.

Think about it: switch Contents list on and keep chatting. What does the model get in place of the old messages? Is a summary as good as the real thing?

8. A picture

Click the paperclip. Attach a picture, or take a photo of something with writing on it. Ask: What does this say?

The missing part is Pictures and sound. Gemini Nano was trained on pictures and recordings as well as words, but it gets yours only if the page hands them over. With the part off, it is told only that a picture was attached.

Think about it: under your picture, open “What Nano made of this picture”. Is the description right? What did it leave out? Then record yourself saying a sentence, and see whether Nano writes it down exactly.

Now go the other way

Choose Everything on in the Parts panel. Now it is a working chatbot. Pick one part, switch it off, and predict what will go wrong before you test it. Scientists call this an ablation: you learn what something does by taking it away.

Two parts are worth a careful look:

  • Rules of thumb. Simple rules written by a programmer help choose the right tool. With them off, the model chooses alone. Does it choose worse?
  • Second look. A second copy of the model checks each answer. Does a model catch its own mistakes?

Three habits of a careful experimenter

  • Run it more than once. The model does not answer the same way twice. One try proves little.
  • Look inside. Every tool chip opens to show what the model was given. The Memory button shows everything it can see.
  • Check the answer somewhere else. A fluent answer and a correct answer look the same.

If there is a coach

Your teacher may have switched on the coach, a much bigger AI that looks over Nano's shoulder. It asks what you think of an answer before it tells you what it thinks. Take that seriously: your own judgment is the thing you are training. You can then talk with the coach about the answer. The coach can be wrong too.

Nano beside the first ChatGPT

The first ChatGPT came out in November 2022. Its model ran in a data center. Gemini Nano runs on your laptop. Which gives better answers?

We kept 30 questions the first ChatGPT was asked, with the answers it gave, and put the same questions to Nano. Read the thirty examples. For each one, decide which answer is best before you open the scores. Then put one of the questions to Nano yourself. Do you get the same answer?

Questions to finish with

  1. Which part made the biggest difference? Which made the least?
  2. Name something the chatbot got right that was really the page's work, not the model's.
  3. The big chatbots use much larger models. Which of these parts do you think they still need?
  4. When a chatbot tells you something, what would you want to see before you believe it?
